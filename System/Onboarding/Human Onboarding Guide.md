# Human Onboarding Guide

Before onboarding, run `python3 System/Scripts/setup.py` from the OS root, then complete both proofs in `System/Onboarding/Connect Your AI.md`. This release ships profiles for **Claude Code and Codex**; other runtimes, including Cowork, are unprofiled and unverified until they have an adapter and evidence. Start your supported runtime in the OS folder and say: *"Read `AGENTS.md` and set this system up for me."* That root entry point sends it through the manifest, Runtime Kernel, Task Router, active profile, and then the routed onboarding procedure.

Onboarding itself takes one relaxed session (or several short ones — progress is saved and resumable at any point). The AI guides you; this tells you what to expect. Stage numbers match the Onboarding Specification.

**1 · Orientation.** First, a quick connection check — your AI proves it can really read *and* write your folder (it reads a token from `CONNECT-CHECK.md`, then writes a test file you confirm in Obsidian and removes it). If it can't write, it's looking at a copy or a read-only mirror, and you'll be pointed to "Connect Your AI" to fix that before going on. Then the AI explains what this system is and isn't, what it's allowed to do, and shows you the kill switch. The one-line version: a second brain stores what you know; this stores what you know, how it connects, and how the work gets done on it. You'll also be shown where to start reading (Product Overview, Quick Start, the Human User Manual) and told that a deeper set is there when you want to understand or extend the system. None of it is required reading, and nothing here waits on it. Nothing is collected yet. You say "go" — or don't.

**2 · Privacy first.** Before any personal data: your defaults. What's private? Do work and personal stay separate? Any topics completely off the table? People whose info needs extra care? Your answers become rules the AI must obey — set before it knows anything about you. You'll also be asked a separate **storage** question: is there anything that must never leave this computer — no cloud sync, no hosted backup? That's its own switch (`sync: local-only`, or just a folder named `Local Only/`), different from `classification: restricted`, which excludes something from compliant AI retrieval. The system checks filtered retrieval and storage markings, but a runtime with direct file access is not physically confined by those switches. Anything that runtime must not technically access stays outside its filesystem reach. And the simplest rule of all: anything you're not comfortable sharing with your AI, just skip — nothing you skip is ever required.

**3 · Who you are.** Your roles — professional and personal. Skip anything; add later.

**4 · What you want from it.** Three to five real recurring jobs, the things you keep re-explaining to AI tools, and where your stuff currently lives (just a list — nothing imported yet; if you later want that material brought in, there's a real, reversible path for it, whether it's a standard knowledge bundle or a whole other vault). Already have a way of organizing notes — PARA, Zettelkasten, daily notes, something you invented? With your permission the AI reads how your existing notes are organized (read-only, nothing changed) and drafts a one-page description of how you work: your words, your tags, your rituals, and a "never touch" list only you can confirm. You correct the draft; from then on it speaks your language. No prior system? This part simply doesn't come up. You'll also be shown where the *work* goes: there's one to-do file at the top of your folder (`To-Do.md`), and from now on "add that to my list" lands there — each item saying what it is, why it matters, what it's holding up, and where to go to finish it, re-checked every time it's opened so nothing quietly rots. You'll be offered, once, to put one of the jobs you just named on it. Say no and nothing is added.

**5 · Making it yours.** The AI proposes small personalizations (which note kinds you'll use, review pace). It suggests; you approve. The core machinery isn't up for restyling — that's what keeps it recoverable. It also asks two questions worth a real answer: do you have rules for **how your writing should sound** (a voice guide, "write like me" notes), and do you have a standard for **how your notes should be built** (depth, citations, when a claim needs a counterargument)? If yes, the AI wires them in — either the rules themselves or a one-line pointer to wherever they already live (a pointer, never a second copy — two copies of a rule quietly become two different rules) — and from then on every draft and every note is held to *your* standard, automatically. If no, the built-in defaults apply, and you can add either one any day later with nothing else to change.

**6 · First real content.** Bring in a few things you already have — a document or two, some notes, maybe a meeting transcript. Then watch what the system does with them: each one gets read, filed in the right place, and connected to what's already there, with its origin recorded. This is the whole system working in miniature, on your real material. Notice what does *not* happen: nothing you bring in is treated as verified fact just because it now lives in the system — raw material stays *staging* until it's been checked, and only then becomes trusted knowledge. (And if you know something about an item that isn't written anywhere, just say it — that gets saved too.) One more thing you'll see if you bring in an AI chat conversation: the system keeps the voices straight. Your notes get built from what *you* said — the AI's side of that old chat is credited as the AI's, or set aside, never filed as your own thinking.

**7 · Prove it works.** Ask three real questions. For supported vault retrieval, the AI names the qualifying records it used and honestly says "not enough information" when the vault lacks support; this is not a promise that every conversational sentence receives a formal citation. Then watch one safe write happen: check-first, smallest change, validated (and journaled when it touches established knowledge). Now you know what its work looks like.

**8 · Done.** Set up your backup — the AI shows you every option (a private GitHub remote, automatic push, an encrypted drive, built-in snapshots, and more) with honest pros and cons, and you pick what fits; the only firm rule is that truly sensitive things stay off any cloud. And backup stays visible afterwards: each copy you make gets recorded (the clone script records it for you; a hand copy takes one command), and the health check will plainly tell you if content that never leaves this machine has no recorded copy anywhere — or how long it's been. It reminds; it never nags, and it never claims you're covered when it can't know. Then pick a weekly maintenance day — that weekly pass surfaces declared relationships, review obligations, and stalled routines for judgment; it does not claim to discover every connection or automatically make every note correct. Everything you've already written is grandfathered. Decide how chatty reviews should be, get a completion report of everything configured and created — and what to do the day you want to change, pause, or leave any of it. You'll also get a plain-language tour of the decisions that shape how your vault behaves — what syncs and what stays private, how it finds and reviews things, the words it uses — and you can change any of the ones that are yours to change, right there, one at a time. The deeper structural rules are shown too, but marked: those keep the system recoverable, so they aren't casual toggles. Anything you change there gets written down as a fresh dated decision in your own `Decisions/` folder — the top-level place your decisions live from now on, the one that answers "what did we decide, and why?" months later; the list you were just walked through is the *product's* own, which updates overwrite, so your choices never get filed there. Change nothing if you like the defaults; it can all be revisited later. And you'll be offered the optional Method Kit — about forty-five minutes that turns how you actually work into your own set of rules your AI follows. Do it now, put it off, or skip it; if you skip it, it's saved as something to come back to, and nothing else waits on it. And you'll hear that your OS can grow later — new abilities arrive as "packs" you add by dropping a file into your main folder, then asking your AI to install it (one already ships in the box, `obsidian-bases`, so you can see how a pack looks); there's nothing to do about that now. The OS itself also improves over time: when a new version ships, the reviewed upgrade workflow preserves declared user/content layers and reports conflicts rather than silently replacing them (`System/Documentation/Upgrading.md` — nothing to do about that now either, just know it's there).
